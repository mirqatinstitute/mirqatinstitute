## What does "Tajweed" mean?

The word *tajweed* comes from the Arabic root *j-w-d*, which means "to make good" or "to do well". In recitation, it means giving every letter of the Qur'an its due — its correct sound, its correct qualities, and the correct way it changes depending on the letters around it.

Reciting with Tajweed protects the meaning of the words (some letters sound alike but change the meaning completely) and preserves the beauty of the recitation as it has been passed down from teacher to student.

## The main areas of Tajweed

### 1. Makharij — where each letter comes from

Every Arabic letter has a *makhraj*, an exact point in the mouth or throat where its sound is produced. The points are grouped into five general areas: the empty space of the mouth and throat, the throat, the tongue, the lips, and the nasal passage. Learning the makharij is what makes letters like ح and ه, or ع and ء, sound clearly different.

### 2. Sifaat — the qualities of each letter

*Sifaat* are the characteristics that give a letter its full sound — for example, whether it is pronounced heavy (full-mouthed) or light, whether the breath flows or stops, and whether it has an echoing bounce (*qalqalah*), as in the letters ق ط ب ج د when they have a sukoon.

### 3. Rules of noon sakinah and tanween

When a noon with sukoon (نْ) or tanween comes before another letter, it is recited in one of four ways:

- **Izhar** — pronounced clearly (before the throat letters).
- **Idgham** — merged into the next letter.
- **Iqlab** — changed to a meem sound with a nasal tone (before ب).
- **Ikhfa** — partly hidden with a nasal sound (before the remaining letters).

### 4. Rules of meem sakinah

A meem with sukoon (مْ) is hidden before ب (*ikhfa shafawi*), merged before another meem (*idgham shafawi*), and pronounced clearly before all other letters (*izhar shafawi*).

### 5. Madd — lengthening

Some vowels are stretched. The basic, natural madd is held for **two counts**; other types of madd, caused by a hamza or a sukoon after the madd letter, are held longer.

### 6. Ghunnah — the nasal sound

A noon or meem with a shaddah (نّ / مّ) is recited with a nasal sound, usually held for about two counts.

### 7. Waqf and ibtida — stopping and starting

Tajweed also covers where to pause, how to stop on a word, and where to begin again, so that the meaning of the verse stays intact.

## How is Tajweed learned?

Tajweed cannot really be learned from reading alone, because it is about sound. The traditional way — and still the best — is to recite to a qualified teacher who listens closely and corrects each mistake until the right pronunciation becomes natural. That is why one-to-one classes work so well for Tajweed: the teacher hears every letter you say.

If you can already read Arabic letters, Tajweed is the natural next step. If you are a complete beginner, start with Noorani Qaida first.
